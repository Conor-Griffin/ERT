Understanding Fire

Fire tetrahedron and combustion

Fire is a result of the combustion process, which is the oxidation of a fuel which produces heat and light, water and carbon dioxide and oxides of various other elements. Since this reaction is exothermic, it self propagates (fire spreads) as the heat produced vaporises more fuel (pyrolysis) which will then burn

Fire tetrahedron consists of the presence of fuel, heat and oxygen, and requires a chemical chain reaction

Fire spread

Fire spreads through conduction, convection, radiation and direct flame contact
  • Conduction – heat transfer through physical contact, is much higher for substances that are good conductors like metal
  • Convection – heat transfer that liquids and gases that involve convection currents, due to heating liquids and gases makes them less dense and they move up, very apparent in building fires where the hot gases go to the upper floors
  • Radiation – heat transfer through electromagnetic waves, can transfer across a vacuum. Radiation may be transmitted (pass through without warming), absorbed (and warmed) or reflected.
  • Direct flame contact – rapid heat transfer to localised area through direct flame contact

Classes of Fire

  • Class A: solid combustibles (wood, paper, fabric)
  • Class B: flammable liquids (gasoline, oil, paint)
  • Class C: flammable gases (propane, butane, methane)
  • Class D: combustible metals (magnesium, titanium, aluminum)
  • Class E: Electrical fires involving live wires
  • Class F: cooking oils and fats

Fire Terminology

  • Flashpoint – lowest temp the substance will ‘flash’ when exposed to ignition source, won’t sustain combustion once ignition source is removed
  • Fire point – lowest temp the substance will produce a ‘fire’ when exposed to ignition source, will sustained combustion once ignition source removed
  • Auto-ignition temp – lowest temp substance will spontaneously ignite without an ignition source
  • Spontaneous combustion - when combustion results from internal heating through rapid oxidation, such as the heat that builds up in an unturned compost heap
  • Neutral plane – indicated by the layer of smoke sitting above clean air, the lower the neutral plane the hotter the room is. Can be identified from exterior by wetting door and observing where it steams off.
  • Flammable/explosive limits – need the right mix of fuel and oxygen in order to burn. Each fuel has it’s own upper (UEL) and lower (LEL) explosive limits. Below the LEL means insufficient fuel, above UEL means too much fuel. In between LEL and UEL is the flammable/explosive range.
  • Pyrolysis – thermal decomposition solid/liquid to a gas/vapour state
  • Flashover – the transition from having a fire in the room to the whole room being on fire, typically happens at around 600deg.C as that is the auto-ignition temperature of CO (a byproduct of incomplete combustion. Indicators of imminent flashover:
    • Rapid increase in temperature
    • Rapid dropping of the neutral plane
    • Rapid pyrolysis of contents of the room
    • Dancing angels – the rollover of unburned combustible gases accumulating in ceiling, can create a sea of flames that look like dancing angels



  • Back draft – introducing oxygen (by opening door or window), rapidly moving an oxygen starved fire from above the UEL to within the explosive range causing an explosion out the window/door. Indicators:
    • Thick, dense, dark smoke – signs of incomplete combustion
    • smoke pulsing from openings, air being drawn in through bottom
    • Whistling/roaring due to fast movement of air through openings
    • Turbulence in neutral plane


Fighting Fire

Methods of extinguishment

The below methods are used often together to extinguish fires, targeting the fire tetrahedron
  • Starvation – limiting fuel by isolating valve or back burning or creating a firebreak, essentially removing matter in the vicinity that is combustible so it runs out
  • Smothering – limiting oxygen, with a fire blanket or closing a door to a room that is on fire
  • Cooling – limiting heat, water is used due to its high specific heat capacity and high latent heat of vaporisation
  • Stopping the chain reaction – intermediate particles are formed in the combustion process called free radicals, by preventing these free radicals from reacting with each other with DCP extinguisher, you interrupt the chain reaction

Fire extinguisher types

  • Follow the PASS technique: Pull, Aim, Squeeze, Sweep
  • Water: for Class A fires only
  • Foam: for Class A and B fires, best option for flammable liquids
  • Dry Chemical Powder: for Class A, B, C, D and E fires
  • CO2: mainly for electrical fires, works best in substations
  • Wet Chemical: for Class F fires

Firefighting Strategies and tactics

Primary size up (OIC) to determine nature and location of emergency, possible HAZMAT, identify number of casualties, obtain local knowledge

Secondary size up (OIC) is a 360 walkaround to identify seat of fire (use thermal imaging camera) and possible entry/exit points, check for casualties on the perimeter.

Defensive firefighting – building is lost/fire is too involved to permit entry so just ‘surround and drown’. If flashover or backdraft is likely, defensive firefighting should be used.

Offensive firefighting – building is deemed safe enough for entry/risk is worth reward, strike teams enter and attack the fire directly and indirectly.

  • Direct attack – spraying stream of water directly onto seat of fire where fuel is
  • Indirect attack – pulsing water into hot gas layer to cool it, using fog spray pattern

Firefighting procedure (RECEO-VES):

  1. Rescue – rescue casualties, first aid
    • Primary search team is to locate casualties and confine the main fire, Secondary search team is to find remaining casualties.
    • Spaces should be searched room by room, floor by floor unless given other information about location of casualties. Either a Right or Left hand sweeping search must be conducted in each room.
  2. Exposures – protection of fire threatened property or fuel sources
  3. Containment – deploying hoses to exterior to prevent fire spread, through windows/doors or through offensive measures
  4. Extinguish – apply appropriate extinguishing media
  5. Overhaul – ensure complete extinguishment of fire and scene safety, use thermal imaging camera to identify hotspots, pull apart and cool
  6. Ventilation – ventilate to improve atmospheric conditions, prevent buildup of combustible gases
    • Natural ventilation – should be performed by first opening downwind exits, then upwind exits if necessary. Ideally windows upwind opened at the lowest point and windows downwind at the highest point.
    • Mechanical ventilation – through fans or through hydraulic ventilation where a cone of water is sprayed from inside to outside through an opening, creating a vacuum inside the room
    • Vertical ventilation – convection currents carry hot gases upwards, upstairs to highest points in building. Making openings above fire, at roof level lets these gases escape
    • Horizontal ventilation – opening windows on 2 opposite sides of a room/building, can be natural or mechanical.
  7. Environment – minimise impact of the fire on the environment, i.e. prevent runoff into waterways
  8. Salvage – saving property involved or adjacent to fire from sustaining further damage

Pump Operations

Pumps may be independent or powered by vehicles engine. In appliance mounted pumps, there is a Power Take Off (PTO) which diverts engine power to the pump, hence the vehicle’s engine needs to be on, and PTO engaged before turning pump on.

When targeting 7bar pressure at the branch, pump operator should account for friction loss:

  • 64mm hose per 30m hose length at 250L/min – 0.2bar
  • 38mm hose per 30m hose length at 250L/min – 1bar
  • 10m elevation gain – 1bar

Water sources:

  • Closed/reticulated water sources – hydrants, provide additional steady pressure
  • Open/static water sources – dams, pools, rivers etc, no additional pressure, requires drafting. This water is unfiltered and salty, can cause issues with pump. Last resort.

Water hammer – occurs when ball valves or branch is turned on and off too quickly and can uncouple or damage hoses

Cavitation - occurs when there is greater output of water than input of water in the pump, which causes formation of bubbles which implode along impellor surface damaging the pump.
